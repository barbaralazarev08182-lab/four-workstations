# 0. Core conclusion

The first hour should **not** try to fully deliver all five long-term product promises.

That would destroy pacing and mystery.

Instead:

- **fully prove** that the room is desirable to inhabit;
- **fully prove** that the open edge leads to an active unknown;
- **fully prove** that real-life effort can physically change the world;
- **seed** the idea that this room will remember what happens here;
- **tease** that Atlas / the room belongs to something larger, without showing the stranger room yet.

Working principle:

> **First hour = 3 proofs + 2 promises.**

The player should finish the first session understanding the product fantasy, while still having major questions.

---

# 1. Curiosity needs a normal baseline first

Do not begin with maximum weirdness.

The player first needs to learn what counts as ordinary in this room.

Early normal baseline:

- residents casually inhabit the room;
- workstations feel used rather than staged;
- an open edge is culturally normal enough to sit beside;
- Atlas is present but quiet/incomplete;
- ordinary props remain ordinary;
- the room is warm, safe, tactile, and calm.

Only after the player has a mental baseline should one small thing violate it.

This protects the emotional contrast:

`familiar -> slightly wrong -> curious`

rather than:

`everything is strange -> nothing is strange`.

---

# 2. What the player should believe after the first hour

The first session succeeds if the player has formed most of these intuitive beliefs without needing lore exposition.

## Belief A — “I like this place.”

The room itself is desirable.

The residents feel like they belong there.

The player can imagine returning to the same room tomorrow.

## Belief B — “The open edge is normal here, but the world beyond it is not.”

The edge should not read as unfinished level geometry or a death pit.

A resident may sit/rest there naturally.

A different edge supports retrieval from an unseen external space.

## Belief C — “Pulling something back is simple, but what comes back is not always predictable.”

The player understands retrieval quickly.

The curiosity is in the result, not in mastering a complicated minigame.

## Belief D — “Doing real work matters to this place.”

A genuine Focus / Commit period produces a persistent, visible change.

The change should be stronger than a number increase.

## Belief E — “The center thing is more than furniture.”

Atlas is still unexplained.

But after one meaningful construction step, it responds in a way that suggests latent function.

## Belief F — “Things I leave here stay part of this room.”

A recovered object remains physically present.

A newly installed Atlas component remains installed.

The first session begins the room's history even before the first major scar/repair event occurs.

## Belief G — “There is clearly more to learn, but I do not need a wiki yet.”

The player has one or two unresolved questions, not twenty systems to memorize.

# 3. Recommended experiential sequence

These are **experience beats**, not locked minutes or quests.

## Beat 1 — Live in the room before solving it

The opening should primarily establish:

- the miniature room;
- the resident(s);
- personal/shared zones;
- the two open edges;
- the incomplete central Atlas base;
- the calm domestic/workshop tone.

No major anomaly should immediately demand attention.

A resident using the room naturally is more valuable than a text box explaining the room.

Strong ambient image:

> One resident works or handles a small object while another can casually sit at an open edge with legs dangling into the unknown.

The player learns an impossible fact before anyone calls it impossible.

### Do not frontload

- world origin;
- room-node terminology;
- Ouroboros;
- stranger-room system;
- five currencies;
- large quest board;
- skill trees;
- giant customization catalog.

---

## Beat 2 — First invitation toward the Outside

The retrieval edge becomes the first unmistakably game-like world invitation.

The player should understand only one proposition:

> **Something can be reached or pulled back from beyond this room.**

The first retrieval result should probably **not** be the game's biggest mystery object.

Recommended emotional order:

`ordinary/useful-ish result -> confidence in the action -> later stranger result`

Why:

If the first thing is maximally impossible before the player understands normal retrieval, there is no contrast.

A first result can simply prove:

- things really exist out there;
- they can become physical room objects;
- the player can keep/place them rather than automatically converting them into currency.

---

## Beat 3 — First small wrongness

After the player understands ordinary handling, introduce **one** subtle object-level impossibility.

Strong current candidate:

### Returning Piece

It looks sufficiently ordinary to keep on a desk/surface.

After being rotated or placed, it later drifts back toward a consistent orientation.

Important:

- no `ANOMALY DETECTED` banner;
- no forced explanation;
- no lore paragraph;
- no immediate puzzle solution.

The strongest reaction is:

> “Did that move?”

The player may notice immediately or only later.

A resident can provide a subtle physical reaction if discoverability needs support, but should not explain the phenomenon.

This is the first proof of the game's `Low Verb / High Discovery` philosophy:

- same simple place/observe behavior;
- new result;
- no new control language.

---

## Beat 4 — Real-life effort creates the first permanent construction change

The first genuine Focus / Commit block should connect to a visible world project.

Do not use a fake 30-second “tutorial Focus” as the main proof if the product claims that real-life effort matters.

The experience should establish:

`I did something meaningful outside the game layer`

->

`the room now physically contains something it did not contain before`.

Strong current candidate:

- Focus contributes enough progress to complete/install one early Atlas construction element;
- the resident physically participates in the construction/installation representation;
- the component remains permanently visible afterward.

This single moment can simultaneously demonstrate:

- real-life effort matters;
- Atlas is a long-term project;
- the room persists;
- player history becomes architecture.

The exact resource model is not part of this document.

---

## Beat 5 — The first Atlas response

After the first meaningful component is installed, Atlas should do **one thing it was not expected to do**.

Not a portal.

Not a holographic map.

Not another room.

Not a lore dump.

A restrained response is stronger:

- a piece moves to an unexpected resting orientation;
- a mechanism settles into a precise configuration;
- material warms/responds briefly;
- a faint mechanical/organic sound occurs;
- a component reacts to the installed/recovered material.

Then it goes quiet again.

The player should understand:

> “We built part of it — but we clearly did not invent everything it is doing.”

This creates the long-term Atlas question without spending the stranger-room revelation in hour one.

Optional deeper layer for attentive players:

> the previously recovered Returning Piece subtly shifts during/after the Atlas response.

This should be a bonus observation, not required onboarding comprehension.

---

# 4. What the first hour should deliberately NOT deliver

The first hour should resist the temptation to prove scope through feature quantity.

Do not require all of the following immediately:

- full research station;
- cultivation system;
- wall crack / repair chain;
- major risk/failure system;
- deep salvage tiers;
- free furniture placement;
- broad shop/catalog;
- complex economy explanation;
- room expansion;
- stranger-room observation;
- incoming-observer state;
- Ouroboros explanation;
- cross-room influence;
- species/history exposition;
- multiple anomalies;
- daily checklist loops.

These may exist later, but frontloading them makes the game feel busier rather than deeper.

---

# 5. First-Five promise coverage in hour one

| Long-term product promise | First-hour treatment | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. “I want to live in this room.” | **FULL PROOF** | Must work immediately or the persistent-world fantasy fails. |
| 2. “Real-life effort changes this place.” | **FULL PROOF** | Core differentiator must be experiential, not marketing text. |
| 3. “I can retrieve unknown things.” | **FULL PROOF** | Gives immediate active game identity beyond office/productivity. |
| 4. “The room remembers.” | **SEED / EARLY PROOF** | Retained object + installed Atlas part begin history; major scar/history chain can happen later. |
| 5. “This room is not alone.” | **TEASE ONLY** | Atlas behaves impossibly, but stranger-room reveal should remain earned. |

This asymmetry is intentional.

Trying to fully prove Promise 5 in the first hour would weaken the long-term mystery.

---

# 6. The first session should leave unresolved physical questions

The strongest return motivation is not an empty progress bar.

The player should ideally leave one or two tangible unresolved things in the room.

Examples:

- the Returning Piece still sits on the desk and its orientation remains unexplained;
- Atlas now contains the player's first installed part but has gone quiet again;
- an inert Closed Seed or similarly unresolved object may exist later in onboarding if pacing allows;
- the retrieval edge clearly suggests that more than one kind of thing can come back.

This creates:

> **unfinished curiosity**

rather than:

> `Daily Tasks Remaining: 3`.

---

# 7. What should happen AFTER the first hour, not inside it

The next sessions/days can progressively introduce the deeper promises.

Possible order, not locked:

1. a second/third recovered thing behaves differently;
2. an object changes across real time;
3. a relationship appears between two previously separate objects;
4. the room itself experiences a small anomaly;
5. resolving it creates the first true architectural scar/history chain;
6. Atlas responses become repeatable enough to look patterned;
7. multiple old clues begin pointing at the same unknown relationship;
8. only much later does an incomplete spatial echo appear;
9. the stranger-room reveal remains a major earned milestone.

The progression should shift from:

`What is this thing?`

to:

`Why are these things related?`

to:

`What kind of world would make all of this true?`

---

# 8. First-hour complexity budget

The first session should expose only a small number of player-facing concepts.

Recommended conceptual budget:

- one home/world;
- one resident identity;
- one open-edge retrieval idea;
- one simple keep/place/inspect relationship;
- one real-life Focus -> world-change relationship;
- one Atlas construction target;
- one subtle impossible reaction.

Everything else can remain latent.

Rule:

> **Do not teach a system because it exists. Teach it only when the player has a reason to care about it.**

# 9. First-hour failure modes

## Failure A — “Cute productivity app”

Symptoms:

- timer/XP/shop dominate the opening;
- the Outside and world mystery arrive too late;
- first screenshots could belong to any focus app.

Fix:

- establish open-edge life and retrieval early;
- make Focus visibly change the world instead of dominating the UI.

## Failure B — “Mystery soup”

Symptoms:

- Atlas reacts;
- wall cracks;
- objects float;
- seed grows;
- strange sound happens;
- three currencies unlock;
- player does not know what normal is.

Fix:

- normal baseline first;
- one impossible event at a time.

## Failure C — “Nothing to actually do”

Symptoms:

- player mostly watches ambient events;
- objects change automatically;
- player waits for future content.

Fix:

- simple controls are fine, but early moments must include meaningful choice/ownership: retrieve, keep, place, contribute, decide what remains in the room.

## Failure D — “Tutorial corridor”

Symptoms:

- every feature is introduced by forced prompts;
- the player performs scripted chores rather than inhabiting a place.

Fix:

- use environmental invitations and resident behavior;
- let the world demonstrate rules before text labels them.

## Failure E — “We spent the best reveal immediately”

Symptoms:

- another room appears in the first tutorial;
- Ouroboros is explained immediately;
- Atlas's final purpose is known from minute one.

Fix:

- first hour ends with Atlas responding, not Atlas explaining itself.

---

# 10. Product test for the first-hour prototype

Do not initially ask testers whether they understood every mechanic.

Ask what they *believe*.

Strong post-session questions:

- What kind of place do you think this room is?
- What do you think exists beyond the edge?
- What did you expect the next retrieval to contain?
- What did your real-life Focus change in the world?
- What do you think Atlas might eventually do?
- Which object or place in the room are you most curious about now?
- If you opened the game tomorrow, what would you check first?

A strong result is not identical answers.

A strong result is:

> players understand the product's basic causal rules while forming different hypotheses about the mystery.

# 11. Current recommended first-session emotional arc

```text
COMFORT
“This is a nice little place.”

    ↓

NORMALIZED IMPOSSIBILITY
“They just sit at that edge like it is normal?”

    ↓

AGENCY
“I can actually pull something back from out there.”

    ↓

SMALL DOUBT
“Did that object move?”

    ↓

OWNERSHIP
“My real Focus actually changed the center structure.”

    ↓

MYSTERY
“Why did Atlas respond by itself?”

    ↓

RETURN INTENT
“I want to see what this becomes / what else is out there.”
```

This is the preferred first-hour shape.

---

# 12. One-sentence first-hour target

> **The player's first session should begin as a comfortable visit to a tiny inhabited room, teach one simple way to reach beyond it, let real effort leave one permanent mark, and end when the room quietly demonstrates that it knows something the player does not.**
