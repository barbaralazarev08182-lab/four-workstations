# VQ2 World Bible v0.1

> Date: 2026-08-25  
> Status: RESEARCH / PROPOSED — DISCUSSION IN PROGRESS  
> Scope: world premise, background rules, mystery structure, inhabitants, Atlas meaning, Outside/edge logic, environmental narrative  
> Purpose: create a coherent world model that can guide gameplay, character design, economy, room expansion, art, animation, and future narrative without prematurely explaining every mystery

---

# 0. Design correction

The project should no longer treat `WORLD_DESIGN.md` as the complete current fiction of the game.

That older document remains useful as historical spatial/product context for the shared-office phase, but the current VQ2 direction is broader:

> The familiar Office is the starting form of a small inhabited world-node. As players live in it, build it, investigate it, and connect it, the place gradually reveals rules that are impossible in an ordinary office.

The room should become stranger through use, not by abruptly replacing the warm miniature-office identity with generic science fiction.

The current visual direction still matters:

- warm;
- miniature;
- architectural-model / toy-house feeling;
- handmade / matte;
- domestic scale;
- soft light;
- restrained technology;
- no cyberpunk neon;
- no glossy futuristic laboratory;
- no conventional outer-space/starfield aesthetic as the default answer.

The strange world should feel like **domestic impossibility**, not a spaceship.

Examples of the desired tension:

- a warm wooden floor simply ends, and a creature can sit on the edge;
- something can be lowered beyond that edge and return with an unknown object;
- a familiar wall can develop an impossible crack;
- a handmade central machine can eventually show another real room;
- the room can grow outward without the player first leaving for a normal second map.

# 1. Research principles applied to Four Workstations

External narrative-design research strongly supports three principles that fit this project.

## 1.1 The world should tell the story

Players spend more time inhabiting and interacting with a game world than watching exposition.

Therefore important background should be expressed through:

- architecture;
- object placement;
- wear and repair;
- growth stages;
- anomalies;
- Atlas behavior;
- strange recovered objects;
- visible changes in a neighboring room;
- environmental reaction to player choices.

Do not rely on large lore dumps to explain why the world matters.

## 1.2 Curiosity can be progression

The player should sometimes act because they want to know:

- what is below/outside the edge;
- what a recovered object does;
- why a wall changed;
- what Atlas is building toward;
- whose room appears in the miniature;
- why the observed room knows it is being watched;
- whether every room is connected;
- what happens if one room affects another.

A discovered answer should often create the next question.

## 1.3 World rules before history

A coherent fictional world first needs stable causal rules.

For example:

- what can cross between rooms;
- what cannot;
- what creates anomalies;
- what observation costs;
- what influence costs;
- what room boundaries mean;
- what Atlas actually changes.

Exact ancient history can remain mysterious much longer.

# 2. Recommended core premise — current strongest proposal

## 2.1 Player-facing premise

The inhabitants live together in a small, warm, partially enclosed room.

To them, the room is simultaneously:

- home;
- workshop;
- workplace;
- shelter;
- laboratory;
- construction site.

It has ordinary-looking furniture and tools, but its physical situation is clearly not ordinary:

- only part of the boundary is walled;
- exposed floor edges are usable rather than treated as construction errors;
- the space beyond those edges does not behave like a normal exterior landscape;
- unknown objects or phenomena can sometimes be reached from the boundary;
- the central Atlas structure can be built far beyond the function of normal furniture.

At the beginning, the game does **not** need to explain why this is possible.

The player should first learn the world by living in it.

## 2.2 The room is a node

Current proposed world rule:

> Each persistent player room is a stable inhabited node inside a much larger structure of rooms.

A node is not merely a multiplayer lobby.

It has its own:

- physical state;
- history;
- inhabitants;
- upgrades;
- Atlas progression;
- repairs;
- discoveries;
- growth objects;
- expansion silhouette;
- traces left by events and player choices.

This supports the design direction that the **room itself** can become a long-lived identity, not only the user account.

## 2.3 The external unknown is not ordinary outer space

The game needs a conceptual name for the space/medium beyond the room, but it should not yet be frozen.

Working design terms:

- `the Outside`;
- `the Between`;
- `the Drift`;
- `the Seam`;
- `the Open`.

Important rule:

> Do not automatically depict this as a black starfield, conventional void, or astronomical space.

It may appear visually quiet, abstract, fog-like, depthless, luminous, architectural, or otherwise difficult to categorize.

Its function is more important than its final lore name:

- room edges can interact with it;
- objects/materials can sometimes be recovered from it;
- signals can cross it;
- anomalies can originate at boundaries;
- room expansion can push into it;
- the global room connection system somehow exists through or beneath it.

# 3. The global ring — current user-stated world topology

This is not an optional lore metaphor. It is part of the current design direction.

Conceptually:

`Room A -> Room B -> Room C -> ... -> Room N -> Room A`

Rules already established by product discussion:

- the linked room belongs to a stranger;
- the stranger is not selected from a friend list;
- adjacency is randomly assigned initially and then persistent;
- the relationship is not reshuffled every session/day;
- the global structure is a closed ring / Ouroboros-like chain;
- first cross-room capability is observation only;
- the observed room knows observation is happening;
- the observed room does not know the observer's identity;
- later progression should allow some form of costly influence;
- influence must never be frictionless/free.

## 3.1 Recommended fiction rule for Atlas

Strong proposal:

> Atlas does not create a random social connection from nothing. It discovers, tunes, stabilizes, or makes legible a connection that already belongs to the deeper topology of the world.

This makes construction meaningful.

Early Atlas:

`unknown handmade project`

Later:

`signal-bearing device`

Later:

`observation instrument`

Later:

`limited cross-node influence mechanism`

The player therefore gradually learns that Atlas is revealing a property of reality rather than merely unlocking a multiplayer menu.

## 3.2 Why the target knows it is being observed

Recommended world rule:

> Observation is an active coupling between two nodes, not passive remote video.

When Room A observes Room B, Room B's own Atlas/boundary/system reacts slightly because the connection is physically active.

This creates a diegetic reason why B knows “someone is looking” without learning A's identity.

Exact presentation remains open:

- a ring turns;
- a small indicator changes;
- a material becomes warm/luminous;
- a subtle sound appears;
- another in-world object reacts.

Avoid a generic surveillance-style UI notification as the only expression.

## 3.3 Conservation principle for later influence

A strong proposed world rule is:

> Meaningful influence across the ring obeys a conservation principle: something must be spent, transferred, destabilized, sacrificed, cooled down, or otherwise given up locally for an effect to cross the link.

This rule solves two problems at once:

- fiction: cross-world effects feel physically meaningful;
- economy: influence becomes a durable late-game sink rather than free spam.

The exact cost is not frozen.

It may involve:

- a specific physical item;
- a processed material;
- Atlas charge;
- temporary local instability;
- a rare discovery;
- opportunity cost / cooldown;
- a combination of systems.

Coins alone should not become the universal solution.

# 4. Four layers of truth

The world bible should always distinguish four information layers.

## Layer A — Objective world rules

These are rules the designers eventually need to know for systemic consistency.

Current proposed examples:

- rooms are persistent nodes;
- room-to-room adjacency is fixed after assignment;
- global topology is closed-ring-like;
- Atlas can reveal an adjacent stranger room;
- observation is detectable by the target;
- influence requires a meaningful cost;
- room boundaries can interact with an external medium;
- player actions can physically alter room state.

Not all objective rules must be revealed to players immediately.

## Layer B — What inhabitants know

The creatures should not begin as omniscient lore experts.

Current recommended starting knowledge:

- they know how to live in and maintain the room;
- they know ordinary room tools and routines;
- they may know that the open edges are normal enough to approach;
- they can discover that certain outside interactions are possible;
- they do not necessarily know the true global topology;
- they do not initially understand Atlas's final purpose;
- they may have incomplete cultural explanations, habits, warnings, or superstitions.

This gives room for discovery without making the inhabitants seem irrational.

## Layer C — What the player knows at the beginning

Very little should be explained explicitly.

A possible opening assumption:

> “This is your shared little room. You work here, live here, and slowly improve it.”

The oddness should emerge through observation and play.

The player first notices facts before receiving explanations.

Examples:

- the floor ends but nobody treats it as immediately lethal;
- a character can sit at the edge;
- an odd object can be recovered;
- a machine in the middle seems buildable;
- a wall does something a wall should not do.

## Layer D — Long-term mysteries

Some questions should remain unresolved for a long time, and some may never receive a single definitive answer.

Examples:

- Were the rooms once one continuous world?
- Were they always separate nodes?
- Who/what established the ring topology?
- Is the ring engineered, biological, architectural, or emergent?
- Where do recovered objects actually come from?
- Why do some objects resemble lost pieces of other rooms while others are completely unfamiliar?
- Is the Outside a place, a medium, a boundary condition, or something the inhabitants cannot perceive correctly?
- What happens conceptually if the chain is followed all the way around?
- Can the ring itself change?

Do not answer these prematurely just to make the lore sound complete.

# 5. Recommended origin mystery — keep two explanations alive

For early/mid game, avoid freezing the world's ultimate origin.

Two especially useful interpretations can coexist through clues.

## Hypothesis A — The Fracture

The rooms may be fragments of something once continuous.

Evidence could include:

- objects that seem cut from larger structures;
- wall/floor materials that align with recovered fragments;
- repeating architectural details in other rooms;
- incomplete systems that appear designed to connect.

If true, Atlas may be rebuilding an older network.

## Hypothesis B — The Organism / System

The rooms may never have been one conventional place.

They could be cells/nodes created as separate units inside a larger unknown system.

Evidence could include:

- repeated node geometry;
- ring-like signal behavior;
- boundary reactions that feel more biological/systemic than architectural;
- recovered objects that do not look like broken room pieces at all.

If true, Atlas may be teaching inhabitants to use a structure that always existed.

## Recommendation

Do not choose between A and B yet.

Early game should support both interpretations.

This gives mystery without requiring contradictory mechanics.

A third “simulation/archive” explanation is possible but currently lower priority because it risks becoming a generic meta/sci-fi reveal and can weaken the handmade miniature-world tone.

# 6. Inhabitants / player avatars

The current character direction is moving away from realistic humans toward unusual creatures partly because:

- the project benefits from a distinct identity;
- simplified/non-human anatomy reduces animation/retargeting burden;
- exaggerated actions can read better in the fixed miniature camera;
- strange inhabitants fit the world's increasingly impossible rules.

## 6.1 Fiction principle

Do not design them merely as “aliens who work in an office.”

Better framing:

> They are residents of these rooms. What looks like an office to us is simply the current form of their habitat/workshop.

This means:

- desk work can include drawing, repairing, sorting, studying, assembling, tuning, cultivating;
- resting can mean curling up, sitting at the edge, lying on the floor;
- tools can be familiar but slightly wrong;
- body shape can justify unusual furniture/interaction conventions.

## 6.2 What remains open

Do not lock yet:

- species name;
- exact anatomy;
- whether every player uses the same species;
- whether rooms contain one species or many;
- reproduction/lifespan/family systems;
- explicit civilization history;
- spoken language;
- whether the player is “the creature” or an external guiding presence.

Character visual design should happen after this world bible establishes enough constraints, but before detailed animation production.

# 7. The room's meaning

The room should gradually shift in meaning over long-term play.

## Early game

`small shared office/workshop/home`

The world feels understandable.

## Early-mid game

`a safe platform with strange edges`

Salvage, growth, unusual events, and boundary behaviors become visible.

## Mid game

`a node under active investigation`

Research equipment, repairs, Atlas progression, and anomalies show that the room participates in a larger reality.

## Mid-late game

`one persistent world among many`

Atlas reveals a fixed stranger room.

The player knows they are not alone, but this is not conventional social discovery.

## Late game

`a capable node that can leave traces beyond itself`

The room can influence its neighbor at a cost, expand physically, and possibly discover more about the ring.

This progression should be visible in architecture, not only UI.

# 8. The Outside / edge gameplay as worldbuilding

The exposed edges are one of the strongest visual/story opportunities in the whole project.

## 8.1 Edge sitting

A creature sitting at the edge tells the player several things without exposition:

- the edge is culturally normal enough to use;
- the inhabitants are not treating the boundary like a normal office safety hazard;
- whatever lies beyond it is part of everyday life;
- the room is not a conventional enclosed building.

## 8.2 Retrieval / salvage

Lowering a line/tool beyond the room should be one of the first actions that converts mystery into gameplay.

The result should not always be a commodity.

Recovered things may be:

- useful;
- useless;
- beautiful;
- dangerous;
- alive;
- incomplete;
- recognizable;
- impossible to identify;
- apparently related to another room;
- a clue rather than a reward.

This is a major environmental narrative channel.

## 8.3 Boundary anomalies

Walls/floor edges can occasionally behave as though the room's “stability” is imperfect.

Possible examples:

- hairline crack where no structural stress explains it;
- sound from behind a wall that should have nothing behind it;
- object partially embedded in boundary material;
- brief loss of normal gravity near an edge;
- material that grows rather than breaks;
- a wall opening that behaves unlike a normal hole.

Do not turn every anomaly into horror.

The emotional target should often be:

`strange -> curious -> risky -> potentially beautiful`

not:

`strange -> monster attack`.

# 9. Atlas as the main mythic object

Atlas should eventually become the most important single object in the room, but it should begin humbly.

Its narrative strength comes from the gap between its initial appearance and eventual function.

Recommended progression:

## Stage 0 — Assembly without certainty

The inhabitants know they are building something, but its full purpose is unclear.

## Stage 1 — Response

It reacts to certain actions/materials/signals.

## Stage 2 — Pattern

The player sees evidence that the responses are structured rather than random.

## Stage 3 — Connection

A miniature image/model/state of another persistent stranger room appears.

This should be a major world revelation.

## Stage 4 — Mutual awareness

Observation itself creates a detectable state in the target room.

The player realizes the connection is not a one-way camera.

## Stage 5 — Influence

The player can pay a meaningful cost to cause a limited effect in the neighboring room.

This should feel like crossing a world boundary, not sending a chat sticker.

## Stage 6+ — Open

Potential later discoveries:

- infer more of the ring;
- understand directionality;
- receive traces from upstream/downstream nodes;
- stabilize or repair chain effects;
- influence topology under rare conditions;
- discover what the ring is for.

None of Stage 6+ is currently approved.

# 10. Mystery-driven progression structure

The world should avoid relying only on quest markers.

A recommended structure is:

`Question -> Action -> Evidence -> Interpretation -> New Question`

Examples:

### Mystery chain: the edge

`What is outside?`
-> lower a line
-> recover an object
-> object contains unfamiliar material
-> research reveals it reacts to Atlas
-> `Why does something from outside know about Atlas?`

### Mystery chain: the neighbor

`What is Atlas showing?`
-> repeated observation
-> notice room state persists over days
-> target reacts when watched
-> `Does that room know about us?`

### Mystery chain: the ring

`Why is this neighbor always the same?`
-> collect signal patterns
-> observe directionality / recurring structure
-> infer a fixed chain
-> `What happens beyond the next room?`

Progression can therefore be partly **knowledge**, not only XP or resources.

# 11. Environmental storytelling channels

The project should prioritize these over large exposition screens.

## Physical traces

- repair patches;
- scorch/wear;
- growth marks;
- taped notes;
- tool placement;
- broken/replaced parts;
- leftover experiment objects;
- construction staging.

## Persistent consequences

- failed experiment leaves residue;
- wall repaired with visible different material;
- Atlas part remains from earlier phase;
- expansion platform has construction history;
- unusual plant permanently changes silhouette.

## Neighbor-room observation

The fixed stranger room is itself a narrative surface.

Without dialogue, the player can infer:

- how developed it is;
- what its residents value;
- whether something unusual happened;
- whether they also built/changed Atlas;
- whether they are currently active.

This is one of the most distinctive narrative opportunities in the project.

## World reactions

Systems should occasionally communicate lore by reacting to player agency.

Examples:

- risky salvage changes boundary state;
- Atlas overuse creates detectable instability;
- cross-world influence creates a trace both rooms can see;
- strange objects alter room behavior.

---

# 12. Emotional tone

The game should not be a horror game, hard sci-fi game, or realistic office simulator.

Recommended emotional mixture:

- 45% cozy familiarity;
- 25% curiosity / wonder;
- 15% gentle absurdity;
- 10% uncertainty / risk;
- 5% melancholy / ancient mystery.

These percentages are design shorthand, not production metrics.

The player should usually feel safe enough to become attached to the room.

The unknown matters because the room feels like home.

If everything is strange from minute one, nothing is strange.

Therefore abnormal elements should be introduced gradually against a stable warm baseline.

---

# 13. Visual worldbuilding rules

## Keep familiar materials

Wood, paper, ceramic, cloth, matte painted metal, warm plastic, frosted glass.

Strange function can emerge from familiar material.

Example:

A handmade wooden/brass object can perform an impossible operation.

This is more distinctive than replacing the room with blue holograms.

## Strange geometry should be selective

Use impossible behavior more than excessive alien ornament.

Examples:

- a clean floor edge with impossible depth;
- a wall crack whose interior does not match wall thickness;
- a small object that unfolds too far;
- Atlas displaying a room with impossible spatial compression;
- a plant growing in geometric stages.

## Preserve miniature readability

All worldbuilding must survive the fixed orthographic/isometric camera.

Large story beats need silhouette/state change, not tiny text detail only.

# 14. Proposed narrative chapters

These are content-planning chapters, not implementation milestones.

## Chapter I — Ordinary Days in an Unordinary Room

Player learns routines, inhabitants, room ownership, Focus mapping, basic interaction.

Oddness is present but understated.

## Chapter II — The Edge Gives Something Back

Retrieval/salvage begins.

The player learns the Outside can produce meaningful objects and consequences.

## Chapter III — Build the Unknown Machine

Atlas construction becomes the shared long-term project.

Signals/anomalies imply the room is not isolated.

## Chapter IV — Someone Else Is There

Atlas reveals the fixed stranger room.

Observation-only.

Target knows it is observed; identities remain hidden.

## Chapter V — The Ring

Player accumulates enough evidence to understand that persistent stranger linkage is part of a larger closed chain.

The exact origin/purpose of the ring remains unknown.

## Chapter VI — Leave a Trace

Costly cross-world influence becomes possible.

The player can affect another room without converting the feature into conventional friend/chat interaction.

## Chapter VII+ — Open

Room expansion, deeper Outside interaction, more complex ring knowledge, larger construction systems.

# 15. Background-history policy

Do not write a complete encyclopedia yet.

For now, historical content should be designed as **recoverable fragments**.

Possible forms:

- unknown object provenance;
- repeated marks/symbols;
- partially understood diagrams;
- Atlas construction clues;
- architectural seams;
- old repairs already present in the room;
- patterns seen in stranger rooms;
- recovered materials whose age/origin conflicts with assumptions.

A history becomes stronger when the player can infer it from consistent traces.

---

# 16. Important anti-patterns

Do not default to:

- “It was all a simulation.”
- “The rooms are spaceships.”
- “A corporation built everything.”
- “Ancient aliens explain every object.”
- “The Outside is just space.”
- “Atlas is simply a portal.”
- long NPC exposition explaining the ring before the player experiences it;
- conventional good-vs-evil war as the main reason the world exists;
- turning every strange event into combat;
- revealing usernames/friend lists and collapsing the anonymous-neighbor idea into ordinary social UI.

These are not impossible forever, but they are poor defaults because they reduce the project's distinctiveness.

---

# 17. Current worldbuilding decisions vs open questions

## Strong current direction

- The Office is a starting visual form, not the limit of the world.
- The room functions as a persistent inhabited node.
- Two exposed sides are meaningful world boundaries, not unfinished architecture.
- The external unknown is interactable but is not yet defined as conventional space/void.
- Atlas is a gradually built world-connection object.
- All rooms conceptually form a persistent closed ring of stranger nodes.
- Neighbor relation is fixed after initial random assignment.
- Observation is the first cross-room capability.
- Observed room knows observation is happening but not who is observing.
- Later influence exists as a design direction and must carry a meaningful cost.
- World mystery should emerge through environment, systems, objects, and persistent consequences.
- Characters may become unusual non-human creatures and should fit this world rather than look like realistic office workers.

## Open / do not freeze yet

- final name of the Outside;
- true origin of rooms;
- whether rooms were once one place;
- who/what made the ring;
- final creature species;
- exact meaning of day/night;
- exact source of recovered objects;
- exact chain directionality from the player's perspective;
- whether deeper traversal is ever possible;
- whether identity can ever be revealed;
- exact cross-world influence verbs;
- whether the ring is visible lore, hidden infrastructure, or both;
- how much of the ultimate mystery receives a canonical answer.

# 18. Next worldbuilding work

Before implementation, the next useful design passes are:

1. **Inhabitant Bible** — creature morphology, culture, needs, behaviors, relationship to room/work.
2. **Outside Bible** — visual/physical rules for edge, salvage, depth, recovered-object categories, anomalies.
3. **Atlas Lore & Capability Ladder** — construction phases mapped to discoveries and world revelations.
4. **Mystery Graph** — major questions, clues, dependencies, and what the player can infer at each stage.
5. **Object Provenance System** — why recovered items exist and how objects tell history without lore dumps.
6. **World Vocabulary / Naming** — names for room-node, Outside, chain, Atlas phenomena, recovered materials.
7. **Narrative Tone Test** — 5–10 short example situations to verify that cozy, strange, risky, and mysterious elements coexist without becoming horror or generic sci-fi.

Do not implement these as gameplay until later discussion promotes specific pieces into a development Gate.
