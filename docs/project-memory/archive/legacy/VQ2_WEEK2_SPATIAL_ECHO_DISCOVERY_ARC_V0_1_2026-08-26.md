# 0. Core conclusion

Week 2 should carry one major epistemic transition:

`Atlas is responding to something`

->

`the response has shape`

->

`the shape repeats`

->

`old physical evidence matches it`

->

`this is probably not an abstract signal`

->

> **“That looks like a place.”**

Week 2 should **stop there**.

Do not immediately reveal:

- another real player;
- a resident moving inside the echo;
- the stranger-room identity;
- observation awareness;
- the Ouroboros topology;
- incoming observation;
- cross-world influence.

Those are later revelations.

The purpose of Week 2 is to let the player independently cross from **pattern recognition** into **spatial interpretation**.

Working rule:

> **Week 1 proves the strange behavior is structured. Week 2 proves the structure is spatial.**

---

# 1. Week 2 is an evidence sequence, not seven calendar days

As with Week 1, “Week 2” is pacing shorthand.

The experience should advance through meaningful exposure and player/world progress rather than consecutive login requirements.

Preferred behavior:

- a player can reach the spatial realization over several sessions;
- absence does not cause missed story content;
- the world does not punish the player for taking longer;
- an active player may progress faster, but cannot reasonably compress every major inference into one short tutorial burst;
- important evidence should recur so one missed subtle event does not break the chain.

The design goal is **familiarity before revelation**.

---

# 2. Starting state — what Week 1 already established

At the beginning of this arc, the player should already believe most of the following:

- the room persists;
- recovered objects can remain meaningful over time;
- the room can develop unusual states;
- past events can leave visible history;
- some unrelated objects/phenomena can share a pattern;
- Atlas has physically grown through real effort/construction;
- Atlas responds in a repeatable way;
- the response is not obviously random malfunction.

The player should **not** yet have enough evidence to say:

> “Atlas is showing another room.”

That sentence must be earned during this arc.

---

# 3. Evidence ladder — every step changes interpretation, not controls

The Week-2 sequence should use approximately five evidence classes.

They do not need to occur as five explicit quests.

## Evidence A — Repetition survives time

A previously seen Atlas pattern returns in a later session.

Its important property is not spectacle.

It is **consistency**.

Examples of the class:

- same impossible resting orientation;
- same three-beat cadence;
- same sequence of moving parts;
- same responsive relationship with a known recovered object.

Player interpretation changes from:

> “That happened once.”

into:

> “Atlas keeps arriving at the same answer.”

This establishes that later geometry is worth trusting.

---

## Evidence B — The first Spatial Echo is barely spatial

Atlas produces something that is no longer well described as a light, rhythm, direction, or number.

It should be incomplete enough that the player cannot immediately name it.

Strong forms include:

- one small plane;
- two connected planes;
- a partial edge;
- a shallow frosted/translucent volume;
- a line with real occlusion/depth behavior;
- an incomplete miniature structural contour.

The emotional target is:

> “What is that shape?”

not yet:

> “That is a room.”

Important visual principle:

> The Echo should feel physically generated by Atlas, not projected as a flat sci-fi screen.

It can rise, unfold, condense, settle, assemble, or become legible through Atlas's own material language.

---

## Evidence C — Old physical evidence suddenly matters again

Week 2 should deliberately reactivate at least one earlier recovered object.

Strongest current candidate:

### `Miniature Floor Fragment`

The player may have previously treated it as:

- a strange architectural scrap;
- a decorative miniature slab;
- an object with no known use.

Now one aspect of the Spatial Echo resembles it:

- proportion;
- edge treatment;
- material layering;
- structural thickness;
- repeated seam geometry;
- another recognizable architectural relationship.

The game should **not** display:

`MATCH FOUND: REMOTE FLOOR 94%`

The desired reaction is:

> “Wait. I have something that looks like that.”

This is one of the project's highest-value content patterns:

> **new knowledge upgrades an old object without changing the old object.**

### Dependency guardrail

Do not make one exact collectible a hard blocker for the main discovery arc.

If the player did not recover the Miniature Floor Fragment, another evidence path can support the same inference later.

The fragment should make recognition richer, not make the story fail without it.

---

## Evidence D — The geometry returns as the same geometry

The first Echo could still be dismissed as an Atlas side effect.

The next major proof is persistence.

Across later observations, the Echo should reconstruct **the same spatial relationships**.

It may become clearer, but it should not randomly redesign itself.

For example, the player recognizes:

- the same corner;
- the same floor boundary;
- the same wall-like plane;
- the same empty region;
- the same object-like mass in approximately the same relationship.

Player interpretation changes from:

> “Atlas makes shapes.”

into:

> “Atlas keeps reconstructing one shape.”

This is the essential bridge from signal to place.

---

## Evidence E — Architecture becomes harder to deny

The Echo now gains just enough structure to trigger ordinary spatial recognition.

Possible recognizable categories:

- a floor plane;
- a wall corner;
- a tabletop-height horizontal form;
- a shelf-like or desk-like mass;
- a doorway-sized negative space;
- another structural proportion that feels inhabited/architectural rather than abstract.

Do not immediately make it a perfect miniature room.

The best state is:

> the player can describe it as a place before they can identify whose place it is.

The first major Week-2 revelation is complete when the player plausibly thinks:

> **“Atlas isn't drawing a symbol. It's resolving somewhere.”**

---

# 4. Recommended progression beats

These are experience beats, not locked sessions or days.

## Beat 1 — Pattern proves it can return

Start Week 2 quietly.

Do not immediately escalate Atlas.

Let an already-known pattern repeat under another ordinary room visit or construction state.

This makes the player trust recurrence before asking them to interpret new evidence.

New system count: **zero**.

---

## Beat 2 — The first shape appears and disappears

Atlas briefly produces one incomplete spatial form.

It should be memorable but insufficient.

Good player reactions:

- “Was that a surface?”
- “It looked like a tiny corner.”
- “Did something just assemble above Atlas?”

Bad presentation:

- tutorial zoom + text label `SPATIAL ECHO`;
- a clean rectangular video feed;
- a complete room silhouette;
- a large UI map;
- obvious human/player identity.

The world should provide the phenomenon before it provides vocabulary.

---

## Beat 3 — The player can compare, not solve

A prior physical object or room phenomenon gives the player a basis for comparison.

This is where `Miniature Floor Fragment`, `Returning Piece`, structural seams, or another earlier clue can become newly relevant.

Important:

The player should not be forced into a “match the shape” puzzle.

The design value is **recognition**, not puzzle dexterity.

The player may choose to:

- bring an old object closer;
- inspect it again;
- preserve it;
- compare it visually;
- simply remember it without interacting.

Agency exists, but the game does not require a bespoke minigame.

---

## Beat 4 — Focus/construction improves legibility, not feature count

Real-life Focus can continue contributing to Atlas physical construction.

Week 2 should demonstrate that additional construction does not merely mean:

`Atlas Lv. 3 -> +15% clarity`

Instead, the Atlas itself changes physically and gains the ability to hold the Echo more coherently.

Conceptually:

`unstable shape`

->

`repeatable shape`

->

`several connected structural surfaces`

The reward is not another menu.

The reward is:

> **the same mystery becoming more legible.**

This is an important anti-feature-bloat principle.

---

## Beat 5 — One feature of the Echo persists across visits

The player recognizes one spatial landmark from a prior observation.

This is crucial.

Without persistence, the Echo reads as procedural spectacle.

With persistence, the player begins treating it as evidence.

Strong effect:

> “That corner was there last time.”

or:

> “That little raised shape is still in the same place.”

No notification is necessary.

---

## Beat 6 — Local things respond to the Echo

One existing room object can react when the spatial form stabilizes.

Candidates:

- Returning Piece shifts;
- Listening Shell opens;
- repaired wall material resonates subtly;
- a previously inert fragment responds.

This is not meant to prove a remote room yet.

It proves that the Echo participates in the same world rules as other established phenomena.

The player now has a network of evidence rather than one special machine effect.

---

## Beat 7 — “That is a place.”

Week 2 should end with the Echo becoming coherent enough to read as architecture.

Recommended maximum revelation:

- a partial floor;
- one or two wall-like planes;
- one persistent furniture/object-like mass;
- enough consistent scale/occlusion to imply a real spatial arrangement.

Do **not** yet show a clearly moving resident.

Do **not** label the origin.

Do **not** reveal another player identity.

Do **not** explain the network.

End question:

> **“If this is a place, where is it?”**

This is a stronger transition into the next arc than immediately answering:

> “Another player lives there.”

---

# 5. Why “place” and “inhabitant” should be separate revelations

Combining both in one moment is efficient but wastes emotional structure.

If Atlas suddenly displays:

- room;
- furniture;
- creature;
- player status;
- observer notification;

then several months of design concepts collapse into one feature unlock.

Separating the revelations gives the project a stronger question ladder:

### Week 1

> “What is Atlas responding to?”

### Week 2

> “Is Atlas resolving a place?”

### Next arc

> “Is something living there?”

### Later

> “Is that another real persistent room?”

### Later

> “Why do we always see the same one?”

### Later

> “Who can see us?”

### Much later

> “What larger structure are all these rooms inside?”

Each answer creates a new question instead of exhausting the mystery.

---

# 6. What should physically exist by the end of Week 2

The room should now contain visible evidence of its own short history.

Possible accumulated state:

- one or more retained recovered objects;
- a Returning Piece with known strange behavior;
- a Closed Seed / slow-change object still developing;
- one repaired wall patch or other persistent trace from Week 1;
- a physically larger/more articulated Atlas;
- at least one Atlas component that clearly came from prior player effort/discovery;
- one object whose meaning has changed because of the Echo;
- an Atlas capable of generating a repeatable partial spatial structure.

This is enough visual density.

Do not fill the room with constant anomaly effects.

The warm ordinary room should remain the dominant image.

---

# 7. Week-2 agency — Low Verb must not become passive watching

The player does not need new controls, but they still need meaningful authorship.

Useful decision categories include:

- which recovered things to keep visible versus commit to another use;
- what old evidence to revisit;
- where to place unusual objects;
- whether to direct current long-term effort toward Atlas versus another room need;
- whether to preserve a strange state long enough to observe it;
- what they personally believe different clues might mean.

The game does not need to certify every hypothesis.

A player saying:

> “I think these two things are connected.”

before the game confirms it is valuable play.

---

# 8. Shared-room enrichment

Week 2 remains fully viable solo.

With multiple members, the same evidence chain becomes socially richer without becoming a group gate.

Examples:

- one member retrieves the Miniature Floor Fragment;
- another later notices it resembles the Echo;
- one player's Focus contribution completes the Atlas structure that stabilizes the shape;
- a member leaves a strange object near Atlas and another later sees it reacting;
- different people form different hypotheses about the same unexplained geometry.

Do not require:

- four simultaneous observers;
- synchronous calibration;
- voting to reveal the Echo;
- one unique clue per person;
- one member's absence blocking discovery.

---

# 9. Anti-patterns for the Spatial Echo arc

Avoid:

## 9.1 Flat-screen reveal

`Atlas -> monitor turns on -> remote room video`

This skips the project's physical world language and makes Atlas feel like a hidden app.

## 9.2 UI solves the inference

Avoid labels such as:

- `REMOTE LOCATION FOUND`;
- `WORLD NODE IDENTIFIED`;
- `PLAYER ROOM CONNECTION 72%`;
- `ROOM B SIGNAL`.

The player's recognition is the reward.

## 9.3 Every clue is Atlas-branded

Do not make every recovered object visibly a machine part or network symbol.

The world must remain broader than Atlas.

## 9.4 Too many new objects

Week 2 should mostly deepen old content.

A new object is justified only if it substantially improves the evidence network.

## 9.5 Echo changes randomly for spectacle

If the geometry constantly randomizes, players cannot infer a persistent place.

Consistency matters more than visual fireworks.

## 9.6 Reveal the living stranger too early

A clearly moving creature is a much larger revelation than a floor plane.

Protect that moment.

---

# 10. Week-2 success test

A successful player should finish this arc with reactions close to:

- “Atlas keeps reconstructing the same geometry.”
- “I swear that old fragment looks like part of this.”
- “That shape isn't random anymore.”
- “It looks architectural.”
- “I think this is somewhere.”
- “I need to know what is there.”

The player should **not** need to know:

- whose place it is;
- whether it is another real player;
- whether the image is current/live;
- why Atlas can resolve it;
- why this specific place is linked;
- that the world forms a ring;
- who can observe whom.

---

# 11. The revelation budget after Week 2

By the end of Week 2, the project should have spent these revelations:

### Already spent

- the room is persistently strange;
- the Outside can return physical things;
- old objects can share rules;
- Atlas is structured rather than random;
- Atlas can resolve repeatable spatial information;
- that information likely describes a place.

### Still protected

- the place is inhabited;
- the inhabitant is another real room's resident;
- the same remote room persists;
- observation is detectable there;
- another unknown room may observe us;
- the network is directional;
- the topology closes into an Ouroboros-like ring;
- influence is possible;
- influence has a conservation cost;
- ultimate world origin.

This protected reserve is essential for long-term discovery pacing.

---

# 12. One-line Week-2 progression

> **Week 2 should turn Atlas from a strange machine that repeats patterns into a physical instrument that repeatedly reconstructs the same incomplete architecture, until the player reaches the conclusion themselves: “this is not a symbol — this is somewhere.”**
